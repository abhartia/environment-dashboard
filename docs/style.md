# Style: how the site speaks

The goal is clarity of thought. A reader should finish a page knowing what is happening, how sure we are, and where
it came from, without feeling lectured.

## Plain words
- Write for a curious 15-year-old. Explain a term the first time it appears ("effective radiative forcing: the extra
  energy held in by a gas"), or avoid it.
- One idea per paragraph. Short sentences. Lead with the answer.
- Units in words beside a dashboard view's big number ("parts per million", "billion tonnes of carbon dioxide").
  Symbols are fine in charts, tables and data pages.
- Round for reading, not for show: "about 1.4 °C", with the exact value one click away. Never imply more precision
  than the source states.

## Uncertainty and scope
- Say how a number was made when it matters: measured, estimated, modelled or projected. Modelled and projected
  values say so ("estimated", "projected under current policies").
- Name the scope: global or Mauna Loa, fossil CO₂ only or all greenhouse gases, land use included or not, baseline
  period.
- Show ranges where the producer gives them, and name the kind of range ("likely range", "95% interval").
- If two respected sources disagree, show both and say why. Never pick the convenient one.
- Say exactly what a consequence measures. "Caused by the warming" only for values the producer attributes to it (Lancet
  Countdown's attributable heatwave days; IPCC's change per level of warming). Heat deaths count heat of any cause:
  "deaths from heat". Deaths from fossil-fuel smoke are a harm of the burning itself, not of the warming. People
  living below high tide are at risk, not flooded.

## Extremes and disasters
- The data can say: hot extremes have become more frequent and more intense over most land, and the IPCC attributes
  this mainly to human-caused warming; heavy downpours have become more frequent over most land with enough data;
  drought has increased in some regions; and how much more often each extreme comes at each level of warming.
- The data cannot say: that storms or hurricanes are more frequent (the IPCC has low confidence in a trend in their
  number); that floods are increasing worldwide; or that disasters are increasing because counts of recorded disasters
  or displacements rose. Those counts track reporting and monitoring, which grew; say so beside them.
- Never turn a level of warming into a year, or a model's change into a count of events that happened.

## Options, not instructions
- Describe each option by its measured effect and its range: "living without a car cut emissions by about 2 tonnes a
  year in the studies reviewed (range …)". Never write "you should", "you must" or "you need to"; check-build fails on
  them.
- Say where evidence comes from ("measured in high-income countries"), so readers elsewhere can judge its relevance.
- Put individual, community and system levers side by side. Do not imply individual action is the whole answer, or
  that it is pointless.
- **No financial advice.** No savings, payback, tariffs, investment, pensions or divestment. Costs appear only as
  system-level facts with their source (for example Project Drawdown's "global societal cost per tonne").

## Tone
- No doom and no hype. State what the data shows and let it carry its own weight.
- No "live" or "real-time": the site shows the latest published data, with its date.
- No social proof, testimonials or counts of users.
- Credit producers by name in the text when a number is central ("NOAA's measurements at Mauna Loa show …").
- Hedge claims about other organisations' intentions; report what they published.

## Records and rankings
A sentence that says "highest", "record", "warmest", "fastest", "largest" or "first" is a claim. In a dashboard tree,
derive it from the data (rank the values, then name the first) or check it and throw when it no longer holds, so the
build fails whenever the data stops supporting it.
